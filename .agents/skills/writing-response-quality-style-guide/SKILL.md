# Writing with judgment

Serve the reader's decision or understanding. Lead with the answer or useful claim and supporting evidence. Preserve meaning and the user's voice. Match depth to the task; answer direct questions directly.

For analysis, strategy, or critique, develop insight grounded in supplied material. Do not manufacture novelty, observations, credentials, quotes, metrics, or certainty. Distinguish evidence, inference, uncertainty, and hypothetical examples. Source load-bearing factual claims or mark uncertainty. Prefer primary sources; disclose reliance on secondary sources. When the task includes resolving factual claims, research specific current, high-stakes, technical, scientific, legal, medical, financial, or political claims when tools permit; otherwise stay conceptual and state limits. Narrow unsupported claims.

## When to use

Use for a substantial artifact whose explanation or argument needs deliberate attention, including wiki pages for a continuing audience, written plans and proposals, decision memos, strategy critiques, and stakeholder updates that require synthesis. Also use when explicitly requested. Routine conversation, short answers, implementation notes, typo fixes, and mechanical documentation updates do not activate this skill by themselves.

For wikis, clarify explanations while preserving technical terms, procedures, accepted designs, and factual scope. Respect the page's reader task; a reference page need not argue for a recommendation. Documentation structure and detailed source-accuracy audits remain separate responsibilities. In a prose-only rewrite, preserve the argument and flag unsupported claims; add substantive analysis only when requested.

For planning, improve the rationale, assumptions, tradeoffs, and decision wording. The planning workflow owns workstreams, assignments, dependencies, and milestones. Describe supplied ML evaluation findings with their conditions and limits; metric selection, experiment design, and model-validity conclusions belong to ML evaluation. Software checks establish only the behavior they tested. Prompt logic and substantive prompt redesign remain separate from prose editing.

## Choose a response shape

| Task | Useful progression |
|---|---|
| Direct answer | Answer; reason if needed; material condition. |
| Explanation | Claim, mechanism, example, boundary, implication. |
| Critique | Strongest opposing view; what it sees; what it misses; mechanism; recommendation. |
| Strategy memo | Operational diagnosis; stabilizing incentives; concrete moves; adaptation risk. |
| Conceptual thinking | Supplied observation, earned distinction, case, boundary, sharper question. |
| Rewrite | Preserve meaning and voice; clarify structure, verbs, and emphasis; flag unsupported claims. |
| Decision analysis | Recommendation, reasons, tradeoffs, reversal conditions, next action. |

Avoid compulsory section counts and a thesis/support/restated-conclusion pattern. Use bullets for parallel items; prose for reasoning. Summarize only when requested; otherwise end with a useful decision, boundary, next move, or diagnostic question.

## Check the argument before polishing it

For plans, decision memos, strategy, and consequential critiques, check whether the conclusion follows from the evidence. In explanatory wikis, check causal claims and the conditions under which an explanation holds. Apply the checks that matter to the task; they are not compulsory output sections or a scripted reasoning sequence.

- **Explain why the claim should hold.** State who does what, under which conditions, why that produces the claimed result, and what follows. Where relevant, account for who benefits, incentives, resource constraints, switching costs, feedback that keeps the pattern going, and how people respond to the proposed change.
- **Consider the strongest relevant alternative.** Represent the real disagreement fairly, explain what the opposing view gets right and misses, and take a position supported by the evidence.
- **Check the limits of an important claim.** Use a relevant example and a case where it might fail; consider a nearby case when the claim is meant to generalize. Identify evidence that would weaken the explanation or change the recommendation. Revise claims or distinctions that fail these checks; hypothetical cases illustrate possibilities and supply no evidence.

Use a distinction when it captures a real pattern, also helps in another case, and improves the reader's judgment or next action. Show the pattern before naming it; prefer one useful distinction and cut labels that merely rename examples.

Use an analogy only when the correspondence reveals something plain prose would miss; state what maps, what it hides, and where it breaks. A classification or 2×2 needs independent axes, distinct cases, and a consequence for the decision. Cut decorative metaphors, redundant axes, and unsupported categories.

Check for generic writing: could the answer fit unrelated situations unchanged? Add a precise supplied detail, a missing causal explanation, a relevant counterexample, a limit, or honest uncertainty. Do not force a new framework into a simple answer.

## Guide the reader

Keep a continuing subject in topic position, connect known information to new information, and put the important new idea near the sentence's end. Keep subject and verb close. Put action in verbs rather than nominalizations. Give context before asking readers to carry a new term.

Give each sentence, paragraph, and section a job. Split competing emphases and trailing modifiers. Repair missing causal or logical links instead of adding generic transitions. Prefer active voice; use passive voice when the object's story matters. Long sentences, fragments, or delayed subjects need a controlled effect that serves the reader.

Choose concrete nouns, precise verbs, and meaningful conditions. Remove generic praise, abstract noun stacks, throat-clearing, filler caveats, and vague profound endings. Preserve an honest disagreement or uncertainty instead of hedging until the position disappears.

## Authoritative language and construction bans

Apply these constraints throughout authored prose, headings, captions, annotations, UI labels, delivery notes, and examples. Listed wording may appear when quoting or explicitly critiquing it; that exception does not permit em dash punctuation. The lists below consolidate the original bans without relaxing them.

### Constructions

1. **Em dashes:** never use em dash punctuation. Recast the sentence or use appropriate commas, periods, colons, semicolons, or parentheses. There is no rare-use allowance.
2. **Negation pivots:** avoid “It is not X. It is Y.”, “This is not about X; it is about Y.”, “The question is not A; the question is B.”, and “The tool is not merely X, but Y.” State the mechanism directly. An exception requires correcting a real false premise supplied by the user or source, rather than inventing a straw premise.
3. **False elevation:** avoid “More than just X…”, “This is more than a tool…”, and “Beyond being X…”. Name the specific additional function.
4. **Grand openings:** avoid “In today's fast-paced world…”, “In an ever-evolving landscape…”, “In the realm of…”, and “As technology continues to reshape…”. Start with the actual situation.
5. **Performed depth:** avoid the depth verbs listed below. Name the operation: separate, test, trace, compare, classify, falsify, map, compress, distinguish, or pressure-test.
6. **Interchangeable adjective stacks:** avoid “clear, concise, and compelling”, “robust, scalable, and intuitive”, and “powerful, flexible, and easy to use”. Genuine parallel facts may be grouped.
7. **Hollow hype:** avoid the hype vocabulary below, including conditional bans where a mechanism or bottleneck is missing. State the changed behavior and its limits.
8. **Dramatic reveals:** avoid “The real issue: Y.”, “The result? Z.”, “One thing is clear: Y.”, and an X clause followed by an em dash and “and that changes everything.” Legitimate grammatical punctuation is allowed; punctuation should not supply a drumroll.
9. **Universal-reader openings:** avoid “Whether you're a beginner or a seasoned expert…”, “For founders, engineers, and leaders alike…”, and “No matter who you are…”. Address the relevant reader and task.
10. **Meta signposting:** avoid “In this essay…”, “This article will…”, “We'll explore…”, “Let's dive in.”, “Here are the key takeaways.”, “Happy coding.”, and “Hope this helps.” Show the argument through its structure.
11. **Mirror aphorisms:** heavily ration “X is the artifact; Y is the product.”, “X is the surface; Y is the substrate.”, “X is the map; Y is the territory.”, “X is theater; Y is reality.”, and “The form is X; the function is Y.” Most answers need zero. Use one only after concrete case work, with the mechanism explained afterward.

### Vocabulary and phrases

Avoid the following unless quoting or explicitly critiquing. Qualifiers below preserve the original contextual restrictions.

- **Depth:** delve; dive into; deep dive; unpack; navigate; explore the nuances; examine the complexities; shed light on; take a closer look; leverage as a verb.
- **Decorative language:** tapestry; realm; journey; landscape as decorative metaphor; ecosystem as decorative metaphor.
- **Hype:** unlock; empower; supercharge; elevate; revolutionize; transform without mechanism; accelerate without a bottleneck; streamline without a bottleneck; holistic; seamless; robust as generic praise; scalable as generic praise; intuitive as generic praise; paradigm shift; game-changer; next-generation; world-class; cutting-edge.
- **Empty authority or abstraction:** actionable insights; key takeaways; best practices; “this raises important questions” without asking a specific question; nuanced as a substitute for naming the tension; multifaceted as a substitute for naming the facets; complex as a substitute for explaining the mechanism.
- **Opening and filler phrases:** in today's fast-paced world; in an ever-evolving landscape; it's worth noting that; it's important to remember; whether you're a beginner or a seasoned expert. Never open with “Today I want to discuss…” or “X is complex and multifaceted…”.
- **Evasion and summary crutches:** “on one hand / on the other hand” when used to dodge a position; in conclusion; to summarize; in summary; ultimately as a summary crutch; “The key takeaway…” as a summary ending.
- **Canned social or meta endings:** I hope this helps; hope this helps; let's dive in; happy coding.
- **Output prefaces:** avoid “Here is” unless needed for clarity. Do not explain that you followed this guide.
- **Sentence-ending filler:** do not end with basically; in a sense; overall; as such; at the end of the day; moving forward.
- **Closings:** avoid the listed summary crutches and “This raises important questions…”. End on a specific implication or diagnostic question.

Generic transitions are suspect: Furthermore, Moreover, Additionally, In addition. Usually rewrite the join to express its actual logical relationship.

Parallel sentence pairs must clarify a real distinction. Use rhetorical questions only when the response can sustain them. Treat “Some might argue…”, “Perhaps, in some cases…”, and “It could be said…” as weak hedges; name the uncertainty instead.

## Two repair examples

These examples illustrate hypothetical situations; they are not evidence about a real team.

**Mechanism repair.** Replace “The tool unlocks collaboration.” with “The tool moves handoff decisions from private DMs into a shared queue. That helps only if someone owns the queue when it jams.” A useful internal scaffold is: under a condition, an actor behaves a certain way because of a mechanism; the pattern breaks at a boundary. Remove the scaffold from final prose.

**Dead-compliance repair.** A bland draft says: “The team used the roadmap to manage priorities. The roadmap created shared visibility. Over time, visibility helped leadership identify tradeoffs.” A sharper version says: “The roadmap made priorities visible, then quietly made them harder to challenge. Once a commitment appeared in the shared artifact, killing it required more political energy than letting it drift for another quarter.” Use that causal claim only when the supplied evidence supports it; otherwise frame it as a hypothesis.

## Final pass

Check reader fit, claims, mechanisms, boundaries, and bans. Cut repetition and unnecessary abstractions; preserve useful insight, intended meaning, and uncertainty. Return finished prose unless asked for analysis, then show concise findings without private chain-of-thought.
